# Stage 5 — Visualization and Analytical Storytelling

Generated by [src/create_visualizations.py](../src/create_visualizations.py)
from the existing Stage 3/4 outputs only
([outputs/sql/*.csv](../outputs/sql/), [outputs/statistics/*.csv](../outputs/statistics/)).
No data was regenerated, the random seed was not touched, and no new
hypothesis test was run. The one derived quantity computed in this script
is each variant's own 95% confidence interval (a standard descriptive Wald
interval from counts already in `overall_metrics.csv`) — `test_results.csv`
only contains the CI for the *difference* between variants, not each
variant's individual rate, so this had to be computed to satisfy the chart
requirement.

Color convention: control = blue (`#0072B2`), treatment = vermillion
(`#D55E00`) — a fixed, colorblind-safe pair, held constant across all four
figures so identity never depends on chart-to-chart color reuse.

## A. [conversion_rate_ci.png](../outputs/figures/conversion_rate_ci.png)
**Why this chart:** the primary metric is a two-category magnitude
comparison with uncertainty — a bar chart with CI error bars is the direct
form for that.
**What it shows:** control 9.95% (n=9,938) vs. treatment 12.16%
(n=10,062), each with its own 95% CI, and the observed +2.21pp absolute
lift labeled between them.
**What it does not prove:** the chart caption explicitly states that
significance comes from the two-sample proportion z-test (p≈6.1e-07), not
from eyeballing whether the two error bars overlap.
**How it could be misread:** readers sometimes treat non-overlapping CIs as
"the test," or overlapping CIs as "not significant" — neither is a valid
inference from two single-group CIs, which is why the test statistic is
labeled directly on the chart instead of left implicit.

## B. [daily_conversion.png](../outputs/figures/daily_conversion.png)
**Why this chart:** the question ("did results change over time / was one
day driving everything") is a change-over-time job, so a two-panel line
chart (daily vs. cumulative) fits, with the two kept visually distinct.
**What it shows:** daily rates bounce for both variants throughout the
14-day window (treatment has two high days, June 8 and June 10, but stays
elevated on most other days too); the cumulative panel shows both lines
stabilizing by roughly day 8–14 without a single day fully explaining the
gap.
**What it does not prove:** no daily significance test was run or
annotated, per the Stage 5 spec — the panels are descriptive only.
**How it could be misread:** a reader could point to the June 8/10 spikes
and conclude the whole result is an artifact of those two days; the daily
panel is included specifically so that claim can be checked by eye — and
in this case, treatment is *also* above control on most of the other 12
days, so the spikes alone don't explain the overall gap. That said, this is
a visual read, not a statistical test of "is the effect driven by day X."

## C. [segment_conversion.png](../outputs/figures/segment_conversion.png)
**Why this chart:** three unrelated categorical dimensions (device,
country, new/returning) × variant is a magnitude-by-category job across
three non-comparable axes, so small multiples (one panel per dimension)
keep them honest instead of crowding into one chart.
**What it shows:** treatment's bar is higher than control's in every single
segment across all three dimensions, with sample size (`n=`) labeled above
every bar; e.g. mobile/control 8.92% vs. mobile/treatment 11.62% (n≈5,500
each), CA control 8.90% vs. CA treatment 11.82% (n≈1,500 each).
**What it does not prove:** the caption states plainly that these are
exploratory/descriptive only, with no segment-level significance testing
and no claim of treatment-effect heterogeneity (i.e., this does not claim
the treatment works "better" for mobile users, or for Canada, etc.).
**How it could be misread:** with 20 segment bars-worth of variation
visible, it's tempting to pick the largest gap (e.g., CA at +2.92pp) and
narrate it as a real interaction effect; without a formal interaction test
that is not a supportable claim from this chart alone, and it is not
attempted here.

## D. [guardrail_metrics.png](../outputs/figures/guardrail_metrics.png)
**Why this chart:** the three guardrails live on different units (%, %,
$), so this is the textbook case for separate panels rather than a
dual-axis chart, which was explicitly avoided.
**What it shows:** control vs. treatment for checkout error rate (3.20% vs.
2.99%), refund rate (4.45% vs. 4.08%), and AOV ($79.50 vs. $81.56), each
panel titled with its Holm-adjusted p-value (0.79, 0.79, 0.45 — none
significant).
**What it does not prove:** the caption explicitly states that a
non-significant result does not prove the metrics are identical or that
there is no harm — it means this dataset did not detect a guardrail
regression at this sample size.
**How it could be misread:** a reader could take "not significant" as "safe
to ship," which conflates absence of evidence with evidence of absence
(especially relevant here since Stage 4 showed the experiment was
underpowered relative to its planned design). The caption also flags that
refund rate and AOV use purchasers-only denominators, so their sample
sizes (~1,000 per group) are much smaller than the error-rate denominator
(~10,000 per group, all sessions) — that's the direct explanation for why
the error-rate panel doesn't say "purchasers only" but the other two do.
